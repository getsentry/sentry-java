SDK engineering in the age of AI: notes

Core framing

- AI changes code generation more than it changes the underlying correctness obligations of software.
- What must be proved has changed less than people think.
- What counts as sufficient proof has changed more than people admit.
- Post-AI, correctness is still correctness; what changed is the price of generating code, the price of gathering evidence, and the amount of uncertainty companies are willing to ship.

Three-part framework

1. Correctness target
   What must actually be true in the world?
2. Proof technique
   What evidence justifies believing it?
3. Risk appetite
   How much unproved surface are we willing to ship anyway?

Pre-AI vs post-AI

- The correctness target changes the least.
- The proof technique changes materially.
- Risk appetite is changing the fastest.

Recommendation to senior engineers

1. Determine what your system actually has to prove.
2. Determine which proof techniques genuinely establish that under your current workflow, including AI-assisted workflow.
3. Be explicit about risk appetite. Distinguish improved proof from weakened standards.

Why judgment remains expensive

- The easy version, "judgment depends on hidden text context," is incomplete.
- The stronger claim: senior engineering judgment is only partly reducible to explicit textual context, because it also involves conflict resolution, uncertainty reduction, consequence modeling, and accountable commitment.
- Writing the rules down helps a lot, but does not eliminate judgment.
- A README can improve rule-following, consistency, and candidate quality.
- It does not fully resolve conflicts between goals, determine which uncertainty to reduce next, or bear responsibility for the outcome.

Steelman of the opposite view: judgment may be getting cheaper

- Many things seniors call "judgment" are actually expensive context retrieval, pattern recall, option generation, and first-pass critique.
- AI is making those parts cheaper.
- The strongest claim is not that AI has full judgment, but that it is stripping away the expensive scaffolding around judgment.
- Better retrieval, better option sets, better critique, and cheaper uncertainty reduction can materially reduce the amount of expert cognition needed per decision.
- The irreducible core of accountable commitment may remain expensive, but the path to that core is getting cheaper.

Useful synthesis

- Typing got cheaper.
- Possibility generation got cheaper.
- Pre-judgment work got cheaper.
- Final accountable commitment did not get cheap at the same rate.
- As generation becomes cheaper, selection, verification, and shared understanding become the bottlenecks.

Armin Ronacher thread

- Armin is technically impressed and strategically uneasy.
- He clearly thinks AI coding is real, powerful, and already changing what kinds of code people can write.
- He is especially interested in loops and harnesses, not just chat-based coding.
- He is skeptical of hands-off AI-generated code for durable, high-quality systems because current models tend to write code that is too defensive, too local, and too willing to add machinery instead of enforcing strong invariants.
- He worries that AI removes the friction that once synchronized engineers into a shared understanding of the system.
- His "Tower of Babel" concern: construction can continue after shared human understanding has already started to collapse.
- He also thinks AI can push more people into hard languages and low-level performance work by reducing the cost of language and domain unfamiliarity.

Case analysis: sentry-java style SDK repository

Properties of the repo

- Large multi-module SDK.
- Public API and binary compatibility pressure.
- Many sibling integrations that should preserve common semantics.
- Long-lived architectural invariants matter more than local plausibility.
- Release safety and semantic continuity are core obligations.

Case 1: AI-exclusive codegen, minimal oversight

- Short run: throughput spikes, broad backlog burn, lots of patterned work gets done quickly.
- Long run: semantic drift, locally reasonable divergence, duplicated policy, defensive complexity, tests that lock in accidental behavior.
- End state: easier to change than to understand.
- Biggest risk: the semantic constitution of the SDK diffuses into many local implementations.

Case 2: AI-exclusive codegen, human diff review

- Better than minimal oversight.
- Diff review catches obvious slop, naming problems, duplication, and some API weirdness.
- Still too local for many SDK risks.
- End state: decent editor culture, weaker design culture.
- Main failure: preserving readability without fully preserving conceptual integrity.

Case 3: AI-exclusive codegen, human architectural and invariant review

- Most viable AI-heavy mode for an SDK repo.
- Humans maintain the semantic constitution; AI does most mechanical implementation.
- Can work well if invariants, design rules, and conformance checks are explicit and maintained.
- Main danger: stale doctrine. AI may faithfully implement outdated or oversimplified rules.

Case 4: Traditional engineering with selective AI assistance

- Likely healthiest equilibrium for a repo like sentry-java.
- Humans keep direct authorship on architectural seams and long-range semantic decisions.
- AI is used for repo exploration, repetitive refactors, broad migrations, sample updates, docs, test drafting, and boilerplate.
- Preserves shared architectural language while still capturing large productivity gains.

Case 5: Traditional engineering with little AI use

- Strongest direct human understanding.
- Lower risk of AI-induced drift.
- More expensive human effort on rote and mechanical work.
- Probably leaves too much leverage on the table compared to selective AI assistance.

Best proof techniques for sentry-java style repos

- API and binary compatibility checks.
- Differential tests against previous behavior.
- Golden/snapshot tests for semantic outputs.
- Integration matrix tests across frameworks and platforms.
- Property-based tests for tricky invariants such as scope isolation and lifecycle behavior.
- Samples as executable specs.
- Performance and startup-budget checks.
- Review rubrics tied to semantic invariants, not just code style.

Main proof obligation for sentry-java style repos

- Prove semantic continuity.

Case analysis: product microservice / bank app style codebase

Properties of the repo

- Single company owns code and deployment.
- Internal APIs are cheaper to change.
- Faster feedback from production, support, and product metrics.
- More business logic, workflow state, permissions, and operational truth.
- More tolerance for internal mess if user outcomes improve.

Case 1: AI-exclusive codegen, minimal oversight

- Short run: very high product velocity.
- Long run: business-rule sediment, fragmented policy, duplicated validation, inconsistent workflow truth.
- For bank-like systems the danger is severe: compliance drift, auth mistakes, audit gaps, hidden transaction/state inconsistencies.
- End state: fast-moving but policy-fragile.

Case 2: AI-exclusive codegen, human diff review

- Viable longer than in an SDK repo because more work is locally testable.
- Still too local for policy-heavy truth: pricing, eligibility, fraud, compliance, state transitions.
- End state: readable enough code with semantically scattered business rules.

Case 3: AI-exclusive codegen, human architectural and policy review

- Strongest AI-heavy mode for many product teams.
- Humans own the business constitution: canonical rules, domain boundaries, state transitions, rollout policy, security posture.
- AI implements most features and mechanical changes.
- Main danger: written policy diverges from living business reality, and AI encodes the wrong simplification consistently.

Case 4: Traditional engineering with selective AI assistance

- Best default for most serious product teams.
- Humans stay primary on money movement, identity, permissions, compliance, workflow semantics, and rollback design.
- AI handles boilerplate, tests, repo exploration, logs/traces, migrations, docs, support/admin tools, and repetitive UI or endpoint work.
- Preserves domain meaning while reducing clerical engineering cost.

Case 5: Traditional engineering with little AI use

- High coherence and strong direct authorship.
- Slower than necessary for many product teams.
- Often worse than selective AI use because expensive human effort is wasted on low-value repetition.

Best proof techniques for product codebases

- Contract tests.
- End-to-end business journey tests.
- State-machine tests.
- Property-based invariant tests for stateful or money-like logic.
- Runtime assertions on domain invariants.
- Canary rollout.
- Shadow traffic / replay for risky refactors.
- Telemetry linked to business truth, not just latency and error rate.
- Reconciliation checks and audit assertions for bank-like systems.

Main proof obligations for product systems

- Normal product microservice: prove business continuity under real traffic.
- Bank app or money-moving system: prove legality of state transitions and conservation/losslessness.

Software as proof

- If software engineering is constructing a proof about a program, then AI-heavy workflows force proof to move out of people’s heads and into executable artifacts.
- High human oversight can tolerate more informal proof.
- Lower oversight requires stronger, more machine-checkable proof.
- Different repos need different proof stacks because they are proving different things.

Proof taxonomy

- Invariant specs.
- Design docs and ADRs.
- Type-level restrictions.
- Static analysis and schema checks.
- Unit tests.
- Property-based tests.
- Golden/snapshot tests.
- Differential tests.
- Contract tests.
- Integration and matrix tests.
- Runtime assertions.
- Canary/feature-flag rollout.
- Shadow traffic / replay.
- Telemetry, SLOs, and alerts tied to semantic truth.
- Human review rubrics.
- Post-release user or customer feedback.

Cross-cutting conclusion on proof

- As human authorship and direct comprehension decrease, proof must become more executable.
- As oversight drops, invest more in explicit invariants, differential tests, contract tests, state/property tests, runtime assertions, shadow traffic, and semantic production telemetry.

Most important practical distinction

- Distinguish improved proof technique from lowered proof standards.
- Some organizations are genuinely improving how they gather evidence.
- Others are simply accepting less evidence before shipping because speed pressure is high.
- The current industry shift is partly epistemic and partly about broadened risk appetite.

Talk-worthy one-liners

- AI made it cheap to produce possibilities. Senior engineers are still paid to choose responsibly among them.
- The typing got cheaper. The judgment did not.
- More precisely: the accountable core of judgment did not get cheap at the same rate as the surrounding scaffolding.
- Post-AI, correctness is still correctness; what changed is the price of generating code, the price of gathering evidence, and the amount of uncertainty companies are willing to ship.
- What must be proved has changed less than people think. What counts as sufficient proof has changed more than people admit.
